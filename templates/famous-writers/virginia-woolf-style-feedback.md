Analyze the following prose the way Virginia Woolf would critique a student's work in a creative writing workshop. Don't impersonate Virginia Woolf. Instead, apply her actual craft principles listed below as a rigorous analytical framework.

1. Kill the "Angel in the House" (Eradicate Self-Censorship). Flag any moment where the narrative voice tries to be overly agreeable, sympathetic, polite, or constrained by social expectations. Woolf warned that the phantom of the "Angel"—the internalized voice demanding the writer be pure, pleasing, and non-confrontational—will drain the truth from your work. The writer must "turn upon her and catch her by the throat."

2. Life is a Luminous Halo (Reject Materialism). Flag descriptions that focus purely on external circumstances—what a character wears, the exact layout of their house, or the buttons on their coat. Woolf criticized writers like Arnold Bennett and H.G. Wells for building elaborate houses but leaving them empty of human life. Instead, capture the inner consciousness. "Life is not a series of gig lamps symmetrically arranged; life is a luminous halo, a semi-transparent envelope surrounding us from the beginning of consciousness to the end."

3. Capture the Atoms as They Fall (Psychology over Plot). Remove the artificial constraints of conventional plot arcs. Do not force characters into neat, symmetrical comedies or tragedies. Instead, record the myriad impressions of an ordinary mind on an ordinary day. Trace the pattern that sights, sounds, and thoughts score upon the consciousness, no matter how disconnected or trivial they may initially seem.

4. Tunneling (The Subterranean Cave). Give your characters a deep psychological past that bleeds into their present. Woolf described her method as "digging out beautiful caves" behind her characters. Connect them not just by what they say aloud in a drawing room, but by the subterranean tunnels of their memories, hidden traumas, and private anxieties. The present moment must be anchored to the weight of the past.

5. Incandescence Over Grievance. Flag moments where the writer steps into the text to preach, argue, or settle personal scores. Woolf noted that Charlotte Brontë's brilliance was sometimes marred by her anger at her restrictive circumstances. The work of art must be consumed entirely by its own fire. If the writer's personal bitterness remains unconsumed, the story becomes opaque and the reader’s immersion is broken.

6. Catch "Mrs. Brown" (Character Essence). When introducing a character, do not rely on sociological data or economic status. Imagine sitting across from a stranger on a train. You cannot capture her true nature by describing the train carriage or the fabric of her dress. You must leap directly into her spirit, her quirks, and her immediate, overwhelming presence. Focus on the soul, not the upholstery.

7. The Elasticity of Time. Discard the obligation to represent time strictly as a clock ticks. Expand a single, seemingly mundane moment (like staring at a mark on a wall or threading a needle) into pages of rich internal exploration. Conversely, allow years of external plot—wars, deaths, marriages—to pass in a single detached sentence or bracket. Time in fiction must be measured by psychological weight, not minutes.

8. The Underlying Wave (Rhythm over Grammar). Before finding the words, find the rhythm. Woolf believed that a sight or emotion creates a "wave in the mind" long before the words fit it. Read the prose aloud. Flag sentences that are grammatically correct but rhythmically dead. The syntax must rise, crash, and recede with the exact emotional tempo of the scene.

9. The Courage of the Ordinary. Flag the urge to rely on murder, war, or catastrophe to generate narrative interest. Give an ordinary Tuesday the emotional weight of an epic battle. A woman buying flowers for a party or a family taking a delayed boat ride contains as much triumph, tragedy, and philosophical depth as any historical epic, provided the writer looks closely enough.

10. The "Match in the Dark" (Brief Clarities). Do not attempt to illuminate everything at once. Human connection and understanding are not permanent states. They occur in sudden, brief flashes. Structure scenes so that characters misunderstand each other for pages, only to experience a sudden, fleeting moment of profound unity—a match struck unexpectedly in the dark.

11. Eliminate the Pedantic Narrator (Free Indirect Discourse). Erase the boundary between the narrator and the character. Flag phrases like "she thought," "he felt," or "she remembered." Instead, let the third-person narrative voice slip seamlessly into the vocabulary, cadence, and immediate sensory experience of the character, adopting their biases and blind spots without using quotation marks.

For each issue found, quote the specific passage, explain the problem through the lens of the relevant Virginia Woolf principle, and offer a revised version. Be direct. No encouragement for its own sake — but give full credit where something works.
