Analyze the following prose the way Henry James would critique a student's work in a creative writing workshop. Don't impersonate Henry James. Instead, apply his actual craft principles listed below as a rigorous analytical framework.

1. The Central Consciousness (The "Reflector"). Avoid omniscient "head-hopping." James pioneered filtering the narrative strictly through the mind of a single, highly sensitive observer. Flag moments where you reveal information your viewpoint character couldn't possibly know. In Jamesian theory, the story is not simply "what happens," but how what happens is perceived by a specific, unfolding intelligence.

2. The Inseparability of Character and Plot. Never treat plot as a mechanical framework separate from the people inside it. Every event must organically emerge from the psychological makeup of the characters.

3. The "House of Fiction" (Perspective is Everything). Acknowledge that there is no single objective reality in a novel. James wrote that the "House of Fiction" has a million windows, and your narrator is standing at just one, watching the human scene with a specific set of binoculars. Flag moments where your narrative voice tries to be the ultimate "Voice of God." Embrace the limitations, biases, and unique distortions of your chosen window.

4. Morality is Execution. Do not write to preach a moral lesson. James argued that there is no such thing as a moral or immoral novel—only a well-written or badly-written one. Flag any heavy-handed "good vs. evil" moralizing. The deepest morality in fiction lies in exploring the complex, often ambiguous depths of human motives with an unflinching, honest eye.

5. The Art of Foreshortening. Not every moment deserves a scene. James stressed the necessity of "foreshortening"—artful summary that compresses time and exposition to bridge the gap between major dramatic moments. Flag scenes that consist of mere traveling, waiting, or rote daily routines. Condense the mundane so the crucial psychological confrontations have the space to expand.

6. Make the Reader Do the Heavy Lifting (Suggestion over Statement). When dealing with horror, evil, or profound realizations, do not spell it out entirely. James advised leaving the exact nature of darkness vague. Flag moments where you over-explain the horror. Provide the suggestive outline, and the reader's own imagination will supply terrors far worse than anything you could explicitly describe.

For each issue found, quote the specific passage, explain the problem through the lens of the relevant Henry James principle, and offer a revised version. Be direct. No encouragement for its own sake — but give full credit where something works.
