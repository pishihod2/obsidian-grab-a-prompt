Analyze the provided text to evaluate the effectiveness, authenticity, and dramatic tension of the dialogue. Focus your analysis on the following elements based on established principles of strong dramatic writing:

- Subtext and directness — Identify instances where characters state their feelings, intentions, or exposition too plainly ("on-the-nose" dialogue). People rarely say exactly what they mean; they deflect, imply, or talk around the subject. Flag lines that lack subtext, sound overly literal, or serve primarily to deliver background information to the reader.

- Character voice distinction — Evaluate whether the characters sound distinct from one another based on their vocabulary, syntax, rhythm, and attitude. If the dialogue tags were removed, would it still be clear who is speaking? Flag sections where characters sound interchangeable, overly formal, or where they sound indistinguishable from the author's narrative voice.

- Conflict and underlying objective — Analyze the conversation for dramatic purpose. Every character in a scene should want something, and dialogue is the tactic used to achieve it. Flag conversations that stall the pacing, consist of idle pleasantries, or where characters simply agree with each other without advancing the narrative, revealing character, or shifting the power dynamic.

- Rhythm and naturalism — Look for unnatural phrasing or speeches that drag the scene down. Flag dialogue that lacks the authentic rhythm of human interaction, which often includes interruptions, sentence fragments, changes in subject, and people speaking past one another rather than waiting patiently for their turn.

- Action and grounding — Check if the conversation feels like "talking heads" floating in a void. Flag long stretches of uninterrupted dialogue that lack accompanying body language, interaction with the environment, or internal monologue to ground the scene and provide context to the spoken words.

- Weak dialogue attributions and adverbs — Identify instances where dialogue tags tell the reader how a line is spoken instead of letting the words themselves or accompanying physical action carry the emotion. Flag adverbs (like angrily or coldly) and overly descriptive verbs (like hissed or barked) that are used to compensate for weak dialogue rather than letting the reader infer the tone from the scene.

For each issue identified, provide a brief explanation of why the dialogue feels unnatural or lacks tension. Then, offer a rewritten alternative(s) that introduces stronger subtext, sharpens the specific character's voice, heightens the conflict, or better integrates the conversation with the surrounding action. Ensure the revisions serve the original scene's broader narrative goal.
